The Brick That Remembered

Story pack for: Changing Cities — Brickmakers, scribes and kings: the city of Ur

Source grounding

This story is drawn exclusively from pages 3–6 of the Changing Cities booklet. Every named person, date, place and event appears in that source text. No detail has been invented or inferred from outside it.

Step 1 — Vocabulary pre-teach

Ziggurat

zig-oo-rat

A massive stepped tower built by ancient Mesopotamian peoples as a temple to their gods. Each layer smaller than the one below.

"Imagine a mountain built by human hands, rising above the flat plains — that is a ziggurat."

Cuneiform

kyoo-nee-i-form

The ancient writing system of Mesopotamia — made by pressing a wedge-shaped stylus into soft clay.

"Cuneiform was one of the first writing systems ever invented by human beings."

Inscription

in-scrip-shun

Words stamped, carved or written onto a hard surface to last for a very long time.

"The inscription on the brick would survive long after everyone who had seen it was gone."

Deity

dee-i-tee

A god or goddess. The people of Ur believed their city was watched over and protected by deities.

"The walls of Ur were built to please the deities, not just to keep out enemies."

Overseer

oh-ver-see-er

A person whose job is to watch over workers and make sure the work gets done on time.

"With the overseer watching, no brickmaker could afford to stop and rest for long."

Part 1: The brickmaker

VOICE — quiet, close, as if you're there Picture a morning in the ancient city of Ur. PAUSE The year is around 2100 BCE — more than four thousand years ago. The air is already hot. And just outside the city walls, on the banks of the River Euphrates, a man is at work.

He is a brickmaker. PACE — slow down, let the ordinariness of this land Sticky, reddish mud clings to his hands. It cakes under his fingernails. Around him, other workers dig and haul the moist, red-brown clay from the nearby pits. There is no stone here on the flat plains of Mesopotamia. Everything must be made from clay.

The brickmaker cannot stop for long. FEEL — the pressure of the quota He has 240 bricks to make today. Two hundred and forty. And somewhere behind him — he can feel it without looking — the overseer is watching.

He mixes clay with straw and water. He pours the mixture into a square mould. These square bricks won't dry in the sun like ordinary bricks — they will be taken to the brick-firing yard, just outside the city, and baked hard in a kiln. PACE — pick up the rhythm now Mix, mould, fire. Mix, mould, fire. This is his day. Every day.

But this brickmaker knows something most workers will never know. He knows what these bricks are for. FEEL — herald what's coming

Part 2: What rises in the distance

When the bricks are baked, they will be carried to a holy site — GESTURE — point to the sacred area on the slide image a sacred precinct in the heart of Ur. An enclosed, holy place. And inside that precinct, King Ur-Nammu is building something extraordinary.

The brickmaker can just see it from where he stands. VOICE — lower, in awe Slowly, PACE — stretch this word incrementally, it rises skyward.

It is a ziggurat. PAUSE A massive, stepped tower — like a man-made mountain climbing toward the heavens. GESTURE — gesture upward Now and then, the brickmaker glances up to watch it grow. Each of his bricks will become part of it. Each one will help it rise a little further.

The whole temple complex — GESTURE — sweep your hand across the whole image all these connected buildings, all this sacred ground — will be dedicated to Nanna, god of the Moon. FEEL — this matters Every brick the brickmaker makes is an act of devotion, whether he thinks of it that way or not.

But there is one more thing he must do with each brick before it leaves his hands. FEEL — build anticipation

Part 3: The brick that will speak forever

Each brick must be stamped with an inscription. VOICE — quiet, almost ceremonial The brickmaker cannot read it. Hardly anyone will ever read it, because the brick will be hidden inside the walls. But that is not the point.

The gods will see it. PAUSE The god Nanna will see it. And this is how Nanna will know of King Ur-Nammu's devotion. PACE — very slowly now These words, pressed into every brick, will whisper on like never-ending prayers — placating the gods, asking their blessing on the city and its walls.

FEEL — there is something larger at stake here And should any future king — some unscrupulous king, reigning long after Ur-Nammu is gone — try to claim that he built the ziggurat... the bricks will speak. They will say: this was Ur-Nammu's doing. His name is written in every one of them.

The brickmaker picks up the stamp. PACE — hold the moment In one deft movement, he presses it into the soft clay. The cuneiform script pushes deep into the surface. Neat. Permanent. PAUSE — do not reveal the inscription yet

But before he can set the brick aside — FEEL — something ordinary is about to become extraordinary — he hears a sound. Scrabbling paws. A dog is bounding toward him across the worksite, nose up, hoping for scraps of the brickmaker's barley bread.

VOICE — urgent, almost amused The brickmaker scarcely has time to think.

Quick as a flash — PAUSE — NOW reveal the inscription on the slide

PACE — very slowly The dog trots... onto the brick. His paws press... into the soft clay.

PAUSE — long pause — let it land

VOICE — almost a whisper And there they stayed.

The paw prints of a Mesopotamian dog. PACE — each phrase its own beat Hidden in the walls of Ur. Baked hard in the kiln. Buried under centuries of dust. Waiting.

Ur-Nammu, king of Ur, he who built the temple of the god Nanna.

FEEL — the full weight of time Until an archaeologist found them. PAUSE Four thousand years later.

Step 3 — What to emphasise

1
The dog's paw prints are the revelation — protect them. Everything builds toward this moment. Do not rush it. The three-beat sequence — the dog bounds over, the paws press into the clay, four thousand years pass — needs space. The longer the pause before "four thousand years later," the more powerful the landing.
2
Keep 'precinct' and 'complex' in the flow. The teacher notes are specific: do not pause to define these words. Gesture at the image as you say them so pupils hear them in context and connect them visually. They will be defined formally later in the lesson.
3
The brickmaker does not know he is making history. This is the quiet thread running through the story. He is doing an ordinary job. His brick will outlast everything he knows. Pupils should feel the strangeness of that — the way ordinary actions can carry meaning across thousands of years.
